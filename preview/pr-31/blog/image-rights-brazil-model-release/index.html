Image Rights in Brazil: What Your Model Release Needs to Say

November 30, 2026 · By Helene Doetsch

In plenty of countries, a model release is a one-page formality someone prints out on the morning of the shoot. In Brazil it’s where a campaign can quietly go wrong, because here a person’s image is protected by the Constitution itself.

We’re producers, not lawyers, so have your legal team check the release for your campaign. But this is what we see on every shoot, and what we wish more brands knew before they arrived.

The law, in plain words

Three pieces of Brazilian law do most of the work:

  • The Constitution, Article 5, X. Privacy, private life, honor and image are inviolable, and anyone whose rights are violated can claim damages.
  • The Civil Code, Article 20. Using someone’s image can be stopped, and damages claimed, if it harms their reputation or if it’s for commercial purposes without their authorization.
  • STJ Súmula 403. Brazil’s Superior Court of Justice settled that when someone’s image is published for commercial purposes without authorization, they don’t have to prove they were harmed to be compensated.

Put simply, if you use someone’s face to sell something without their permission, they can claim damages, and they don’t need to show it hurt them.

What a release has to cover

A good release in Brazil is specific. A vague “all media, forever” line is exactly what you want to avoid. At minimum it should say:

  • How long. The usage period. Six months to a year is typical.
  • Which media. Social media and TV are different things, and so are online ads, print and out-of-home. Name them.
  • Which territory. Brazil only, a region, or worldwide.
  • Reposting. Whether you can keep posting new content from the shoot later, say a year on, and how often the talent can repost it or use it in their portfolio.

When the usage period ends, you can renegotiate. A renewal usually runs at 20% to 30% of the original image release fee.

Usage terms are part of the deal with the talent, and TV really does make a difference. Decide where the campaign will run before casting, not after.

What happens when a brand gets it wrong

Best case, you’re asked to take the images down, which hurts enough mid-campaign. Worst case, if it was handled badly, the brand gets sued. And as the STJ ruling above makes clear, the person doesn’t have to prove they were harmed.

The people you didn’t cast

This is the part foreign crews miss most. You can’t just film anyone in public. Brazil is strict about image rights, so people who are recognizable on camera need to give their consent, and locations need the right clearances too.

On a busy beach or in a favela, plan for it. Know which shots will have bystanders in them, and have someone on the ground whose job is getting consent and paperwork while you shoot. When we cast from communities, like we did for Nike × NOCTA, the releases are part of the casting, not an afterthought. We’ve written more about doing this respectfully in Shooting in the Favela.

Why we sort it out first

There are a lot of things to take into account, and it’s one of the main reasons to work with a local partner. We prepare releases with the casting, check them against where the campaign will run, and sort out consent before anyone picks up a camera, so nobody’s chasing signatures after the shoot.

If you send us your media plan with the brief, we’ll build the releases around it. And if you’re still working out the rest, our Filming in Brazil FAQ covers permits, ANCINE and timelines.
